Sinribe · how it is built · for programmers

Inside Sinribe

Sinribe turns a recording, or a link to one, into a transcript that knows who said each sentence. If you ask, it also writes a brief that teaches the recording in minutes and checks its claims against today's web.

This page explains it four times, each time closer. Stop at the depth you need: level 1 tells you what it does, level 2 lets you follow one job, level 3 is for changing a stage, and level 4 is for debugging one. The reasons behind the design are collected at the end.

The published benchmark scores the same interview with its own scorer, which keeps filler words, so its reference has 9,174 words instead of 8,980. It reports 18.84 % against Vibe's 20.51 %, and 99.60 % of words on the right speaker. The absolute numbers differ, and Sinribe leads in both.

What it is not

  • Not live dictation. It works on finished recordings, ten seconds or four hours long.
  • Not a cloud service. There is no server and no account, apart from the optional Claude login for the brief.
  • Not a finished proofread. On hard, distant, accented speech about one word in five is still wrong. The transcript ends with a list of exactly where to listen again.

Level 2 of 4 · sinribe/pipeline.py, Runner.run()

How one job flows

Open the box and there are ten stages in a fixed order, running in three places: on the network, on the GPU, and on the CPU. The dotted line is the most important design decision on this page.

Fail at second zero

The brief's writer is checked before any audio is decoded. A missing Claude login, or a web check asked of a model that cannot search, stops the job with a dialog, not after a two-hour wait. The check reads the stored login with claude auth status and costs nothing.

Protect the transcript

Every stage left of the line saves a checkpoint, and only a finished stage may save one. A cancelled or crashed job resumes where it stopped. Any failure in the brief is logged and shown, and the transcript files are already on disk.

Level 3 of 4 · one stage at a time

How each part works

Six parts, each with its mechanism drawn out and the reason it is built that way. The numbers are measurements from real runs. Where something has not been measured, the text says so.

sinribe/fetch.py · probe() and download_audio()

Getting the audio

fetch.py is the only networked module in the transcription half. It wraps yt-dlp, so YouTube is the common case but any site yt-dlp supports works. It reads the metadata first without downloading, so the Check button can show title, channel and length before you commit to three hours of audio.

WHY

No re-encoding. The decode stage normalises everything to 16 kHz mono anyway, so re-encoding the download would only add a lossy generation.

The publish date is kept. A claim can only be outdated relative to when it was made, so the web check needs it. Description and chapter markers are kept too, because podcasts list the papers and tools they mention there.

yt-dlp is deliberately unpinned. Sites change their delivery constantly and a pinned copy rots silently. If a link stops working, update it first: uv pip install --python .venv/bin/python -U yt-dlp.

sinribe/merge.py · workers/diar_worker.py · workers/refine_worker.py

Who said it

Two models look at the same audio and neither knows the whole answer. The diarizer (pyannote) hears when the voice changes across the whole recording, but its boundaries run late. Whisper hears what was said, in segments that ignore who is speaking. Joining the two is where transcripts usually go wrong. Sinribe joins them in four steps:

  1. Each word goes to the diarization turn it overlaps most.
  2. Each sentence takes the majority speaker of its words, weighted by how long each word lasts.
  3. A voice-print check may overrule a sentence, but only when it is decisive.
  4. Runs of fewer than 3 words are smoothed into their neighbours, then words are grouped into turns. A turn breaks on a speaker change, a silence over 1.5 s, or past 1,200 characters.

Questions merged into their answers, one 34-minute German interview (fewer is better)

word by word28
+ sentence vote18
+ voice prints11
WHY

A sentence is the right unit. A single word is too small to weigh against a boundary that routinely lands 300 ms late, and a whole whisper segment often spans two speakers. People take turns at sentence boundaries.

The voice print is the only fix for a span labelled outright wrong. By then every word carries the wrong label, so no regrouping can recover it. Margins on real audio are bimodal: confident corrections land above 0.3 and coin flips below 0.1, so the 0.15 threshold sits in an empty gap. It costs about 5 seconds on a 34-minute file, because only the embedding model is loaded.

sinribe/rover.py · sinribe/presets.py · workers/asr_worker.py

Getting the words right

A single whisper pass makes mistakes that depend on how it happened to cut the audio into chunks. Decode the same audio differently, or with a differently trained model, and it mishears other words. So the default setting transcribes three times and lets every word be decided by vote. The first pass is the pivot: it supplies the timings, and the others can only overrule its text.

That is the mechanism. The quality slider in the window turns it into a choice between speed and accuracy, and each position shows the error rate it actually scored on a hard German interview, not an adjective.

WHY

Vote rather than search harder. Beam search explores one model's own guesses, so it only rescues an answer the model already had. On this audio the model's probabilities are themselves wrong about whether it heard das or es, and searching a wrong distribution harder returns the same wrong answer at five times the price.

The biggest single step is a different model. Adding the turbo-german pass takes 20.8 % to 20.4 %. Two models trained differently mishear different words; one model re-chunked mostly mishears the same ones.

Vanilla large-v3 is the default. The German fine-tune scored 24.8 % against 21.3 % on this interview. Its published gains come from read speech, and the narrower training costs robustness on distant, accented, spontaneous speech.

The slow positions show no number on purpose. Their old figures were measured while a bug fed the noise-filtered passes unfiltered audio, so they voted with copies of the first pass. The bug is fixed; the numbers come back only after a new measurement.

The last few points are left to a human. 8.7 % of words are wrong in every configuration tried, so no setting reaches them. Instead the transcript ends with Worth a listen: the passages below 0.5 confidence, which are 5 % of the text and contain words that are wrong 57 % of the time.

sinribe/summary/build.py · summarize()

Writing the brief

The brief is built in several bounded calls instead of one big ask. A single call that has to invent the outline and fill it writes a strong first section and a tired last one. Planning first means each section is written with the whole outline in view, so sections stay distinct. With the web check on, the plan also lists the claims worth checking, and the sections are written after the verdicts exist, so a stale fact is corrected where it is mentioned.

The summary never sees audio. It reads the merged turns, one line per turn with a timestamp, plus a short source block: title, channel, publish date and its age in days, length, speakers, language and today's date.

WHY

A word budget, because the brief has to beat listening. Without one, a 10 min 45 s video came back as a 13-minute read. Now the full summary gets about 15 % of the spoken words, the same video reads in 3 minutes, and a two-hour interview in about a quarter of an hour.

sections = max(3, min(12, minutes // 10 + 2))
total    = clamp(0.15 × spoken_words, 400, 3000)
budget   = clamp(total // sections, 150, 350)   # words per section
summary/build.py _web_check() · summary/schema.py verdicts(), restrict_links()

Checking it against today

The question this answers is the one you have about any AI video older than a few weeks: which of its facts no longer hold? It runs in four steps.

  1. Pick the claims. The plan pass has just read the whole transcript, so it lists up to 16 specific, checkable claims, time-sensitive ones first: model versions, benchmarks, prices, release dates, company plans, predictions with a date.
  2. Search. The research call knows the publish date, prefers primary sources newer than the video, treats AI facts as fast-changing, and may not call a claim outdated on an undated source alone.
  3. Verify. A call with no tools turns the report into one verdict per claim: outdated, incorrect, disputed, still open (a prediction not yet due), still accurate or unverified.
  4. Write with it. Each section gets the verdicts for its own claims and states both what the recording said and what is true now.
WHY

Sources come from the tools, never from the model's text. A model can name a plausible source it never read. The research call runs with stream-json output so the code can read each tool result itself, and only those URLs count.

Research gets its own call, and verification has no tools. Searching and judging are separate jobs. The verify call can only weigh what the research call actually found, and its output is shaped to a schema the code can filter.

  • DATES

    Claude Code's search results carry no dates. The "as of" date comes from what the model read on the pages it opened.

  • JUDGEMENT

    A verdict is still a model's reading of its sources. The allow-list proves a source was really returned, not that it says what the verdict claims. Treat "outdated" as a strong pointer and open the link before acting on it.

  • API

    The direct-API research path has only run against a stub, never the real API. The Claude Code path is the one verified end to end.

summary/schema.py · render/webpage.py

The page you read

Nothing the model returns goes straight to the page. Every answer is repaired if it was cut off, clamped to the schema's limits, cleaned of en and em dashes, held to the link allow-list, and only then escaped and rendered. The order on the page is the order a learner needs: the idea, what has changed, the vocabulary, the detail, then a way to check it stuck.

WHY

One self-contained file. Inline CSS, no JavaScript, no web fonts, light and dark themes, print rules. It opens from a file:// path on a machine with no network, years from now.

Outdated claims are flagged in four places. The top banner, the 1-minute version, "What has changed", and the section where the claim comes up. A reader who stops after one minute is still warned.

The machinery

Underneath the stages: which process runs what and in which Python environment, what lands on disk, what the data looks like between stages, how the Claude calls are fenced in, and what happens when any of it breaks.

sinribe/pipeline.py _run_worker() · sinribe/workers/ · sinribe-run

Processes and environments

The window and the job's orchestrator share one Python process. Every model runs in a child process of its own, started when its stage begins and gone when it ends. Two different Python environments are involved, and that is not a preference.

WHY

Two environments are forced. faster-whisper's engine, ctranslate2, needs libcublas.so.12. PyTorch 2.13 from PyPI is a CUDA 13 build. They cannot be installed into one environment. Worse, the cuDNN wheels for CUDA 12 and 13 both ship a file called libcudnn.so.9, so a CUDA 13 worker that inherits the CUDA 12 library path loads the wrong one. Hence the stripped variable.

One process per model pays for itself. GPU memory, including the CUDA context, is only truly returned when a process exits. So the peak is the larger stage, not the sum of all stages, which is what lets this run on a 12 GB card that also hosts Ollama. An out-of-memory crash cannot take the window down, and Cancel is a plain kill with no shutdown race.

It degrades instead of failing. The speech worker starts at the precision free memory allows and steps down one tier on an out-of-memory error, rather than dying eight minutes into a four-hour file.

sinribe/pipeline.py _job_key() and the checkpoint reads

What lands on disk

Each recording gets a job folder named after a hash of its path, size and modification time. Every checkpoint stores the settings that produced it, so changing a setting recomputes only what that setting affects.

WHY

Resume is free, so it has to be honest. A finished four-hour job comes back in 1.3 s. That speed once leaked into the reported numbers: a run at the 10.7× setting printed 29.5× because it read three passes from cache. Now the runner counts which passes it actually decoded, prints a speed only when all of them ran, and lists what it reused.

A killed stage writes nothing. An early bug saved a checkpoint from a cancelled diarization, and the resumed job silently produced a transcript with no speaker separation at all.

The data between stages

Four small dataclasses carry the transcription half. Everything after the merge is a plain dict, which is also what the sidecar JSON stores, so renaming a speaker and re-exporting never touches the GPU.

WHY

Claude Code is the default because it needs no key. It is the same login you already use, billed as subscription usage. The API path stays for anyone who has a key and wants the output shape enforced by the decoder.

It is found outside PATH on purpose. An npm install with a user prefix puts claude in ~/.npm-global/bin, which only ~/.bashrc adds to PATH. Started from the app menu, Sinribe could not see it, and "auto" quietly fell back to a 4-billion-parameter local model. The lookup now checks the installers' folders after PATH, and a fallback is never silent again.

the decisions, in one place

Why it is built like this

Every unusual choice above has one of four kinds of reason. Some were forced by the libraries or the hardware. Some were settled by measuring the alternatives. Some come from failures seen in real runs, without a benchmark behind them. The rest are deliberate policies about privacy, honesty and convenience.

FORCEDno real alternative MEASUREDalternatives scored OBSERVEDseen failing in real runs POLICYchosen on principle
FORCED

Two Python environments, one process per GPU model

Instead of one environment and threads.

ctranslate2 needs CUDA 12, PyTorch ships CUDA 13, and their cuDNN libraries share a file name. The split also returns GPU memory between stages, isolates crashes, and makes Cancel a kill.

POLICY

Transcription never leaves the machine

Instead of a cloud speech API.

Recordings are often private interviews. The speech workers run with the model hub forced offline; only the link download and the optional brief use the network, and the brief can run locally too.

MEASURED

Speakers decided per sentence, then checked by voice

Instead of per word or per whisper segment.

Questions merged into answers fell from 28 to 18 with the sentence vote and to 11 with voice prints, on a 34-minute interview, for about 5 seconds of extra work.

MEASURED

Vote across passes and models

Instead of a wider beam search.

Three voted passes reach 20.4 % against 21.3 % for one. Beam 20 costs 5.6× the compute and scores 21.6 %. The biggest step is adding a differently trained model.

MEASURED

Vanilla large-v3 by default

Instead of the German fine-tune.

On far-field Bavarian speech the fine-tune scored 24.8 % against 21.3 %. It stays installable for clean, close-miked German, where it may win.

MEASURED

Point at doubtful words instead of chasing the floor

Instead of ever more passes.

8.7 % of words are wrong in every configuration tried. But 57 % of the words below 0.5 confidence are wrong, so listing those passages turns proofreading into a checklist.

POLICY

Every number the app prints is a measurement

Instead of wall clock and simulations.

Speed is transcription only and suppressed when passes came from cache. Slider figures produced under a bug were withdrawn rather than kept. A number the app prints is a claim the user will check.

POLICY

The transcript is saved before the brief starts

Instead of one job that succeeds or fails as a whole.

The brief depends on the network and a model; the transcript is the job you asked for. A brief failure is shown, and the transcript files are already on disk.

OBSERVED

Check the brief's writer at second zero

Instead of discovering it after transcription.

A missing login found after two hours of transcription wastes the two hours. The check reads the stored login and costs nothing.

POLICY

Claude Code as the default writer

Instead of requiring an API key.

It uses the login already on the machine, billed as subscription usage. One resumed session per job reads the transcript from cache: 2 input tokens against 11,677 cached on a resumed turn.

OBSERVED

Plan first, then one call per section

Instead of one call for the whole brief.

One long call writes a strong first section and a tired last one. With the outline in view, each section stays distinct, and verdicts can arrive before the prose is written.

MEASURED

A word budget for the brief

Instead of letting the model decide the length.

Without it, a 10-minute video produced a 13-minute read, longer than listening. At 15 % of the spoken words the same video reads in 3 minutes.

POLICY

Sources only from what the search returned

Instead of URLs the model writes.

A model can name a plausible source it never read. Tool results are read from the stream; a verdict without such a source becomes "unverified", and links in the prose meet the same rule.

OBSERVED

Find the CLI beyond PATH, and never fall back silently

Instead of trusting the desktop session's PATH.

From the app menu, claude looked uninstalled and "auto" used a small local model without a word. Now the install folders are searched, and any fallback is logged, asked about and printed in the brief.
